## Related works

The wave-elevator coupling we study sits between several established bodies of work, each touching on a piece of our setting without addressing the joint problem. We position our contribution against the literature in three steps: first we summarize the broader warehouse-OR landscape through three recent surveys; then we walk through the four problem-class threads whose intersection defines our gap (multi-story AMR and elevator group control, wave and order batching as a tactical decision, tier-captive multi-story RMFS, and data-driven fleet coordination), reading each thread for what it supplies and what it omits relative to our setting. Finally, we summarize three methodological strands on which our two analytical tools rest, with precise equivalences established in Section 4.

Three surveys frame the landscape. Boysen et al. (2019) survey warehousing in the e-commerce era, identifying the storage-and-retrieval challenges that motivate vertically stacked fulfilment buildings; Azadeh, De Koster, et al. (2019) review robotized, and automated warehouse systems with a dedicated treatment of robotic mobile fulfilment; and Pardo et al. (2024) provide the most recent taxonomy of the order-batching family of problems, classifying decisions by their strategic, tactical, or operational layer. Within the scope of these surveys, we have not found the multi-story wave–elevator coupling treated as a standalone problem class, consistent with our positioning of this work as a formalization step.

The physical infrastructure of our setting, floors connected by shared freight elevators, has been studied piecewise in the literature we surveyed, with the pieces rarely brought together. Multi-agent robot-lift scheduling on a fixed task set is the closest precedent at the elevator layer: Chakravarty et al. (2025) compute provably optimal lift schedules for AMR fleets via Boolean satisfiability, achieving exact optimality on hotel and hospital deployments. Their formulation takes the robot–task assignment as exogenous and finds the time-optimal schedule on a fixed task set; in our setting, that fix is what we must decide, because the composition of the task set determines downstream elevator concurrency and is the dominant tactical lever for makespan. We treat wave composition as an upstream decision variable that generates the task set their methods would then optimally schedule, with the two layers coupled through shared elevator capacity. The elevator subsystem itself has a parallel literature in elevator group control: Crites et al. (1998) remain the canonical reference for learned dispatch policies under stochastic passenger arrivals, and Tsai et al. (2025) extend the family to standby strategies in smart buildings. These works optimize the elevator dispatch given an exogenous arrival process; we optimize that process by choosing the wave that generates it. At the storage layer, Azadeh, Roy, et al. (2019) analyze vertical robotic storage and retrieval systems, but the vertical degree of freedom there is robot-internal rather than building-shared. Multi-story robotic mobile fulfilment provides a complementary thread: Z. Wu et al. (2024) address inbound job scheduling in four-way shuttle systems, and Tadumadze et al. (2023) study order and pod assignment in multi-level RMFS. Both adopt a tier-captive transport model in which each shuttle or pod is assigned to a specific layer, so layer-level operations decouple, and wave composition does not appear as a decision variable. In our setting, by contrast, flexible AMRs queue at a shared elevator pool, so orders released to one floor consume vertical capacity that orders on other floors are competing for; the layers therefore couple through the shared elevator, and a layer-by-layer decomposition is no longer adequate.

Treating the composition of an order batch as a decision variable, not merely its size, has a long-standing tradition in warehouse operations research. The studies we reviewed in this area primarily focus on single-floor scenarios. Gademann et al. (2001) introduced wave picking in parallel-aisle warehouses with a makespan objective, the closest precedent to ours in objective function; Bozer & Kile (2008) and the canonical treatment of Bartholdi & Hackman (2019) embed the decision in walk-and-pick systems. Ardjmand et al. (2018) minimize order-picking makespan with multiple pickers under wave picking, an objective identical to ours, but on a single floor without a shared vertical resource. Rasmi et al. (2022) extend wave picking to the mixed-shelves storage strategy in e-commerce warehouses; Schiffer et al. (2022) derive optimal picking policies in the same e-commerce setting; Haouassi et al. (2022) integrate order line batching with batch scheduling and picker routing under split-order flexibility. Modern AMR-assisted variants enlarge the decision class: Scholz et al. (2017) formalize the Joint Order Batching, Assignment, Sequencing, and Picker Routing Problem; Žulj et al. (2022) lift it to AMR-assisted picker-to-parts systems; Qin et al. (2024) study multi-tote AMR fulfilment and identify an empirical wave-size optimum near 100 orders, where wave size is a cardinality parameter rather than a composition decision. Two device-level batching threads are adjacent rather than parent: Nicolas et al. (2018) formulate order batching for vertical lift modules where each module is a single mechanical device and the decision is which order lines to retrieve in one tray load, and Boysen et al. (2018) address the minimum order spread sequencing problem in automated sorting; the mathematics in both is internal to the device and does not generalize to a multi-elevator capacity shared across a horizontal AMR fleet. The Pardo et al. (2024) taxonomy classifies all of these works at the tactical layer of order-batching decisions, and our wave-composition decision belongs to the same layer; what is new in our setting is that the tactical decision is evaluated against a shared vertical resource that serves a building-wide AMR fleet. We extend this decision class to the multi-story setting and represent wave composition through Φ=(C, I, T), where each axis captures a distinct vertical-resource stress.

A parallel research stream addresses warehouse fleet control through learning rather than formal optimization, and we summarize it here to position our contribution as complementary rather than competitive. Crites et al. (1998), already mentioned for elevator group control, established the foundational reinforcement-learning treatment of vertical-resource scheduling; the line continues today in AMR-specific work. Ma et al. (2025) propose an end-to-end deep reinforcement-learning framework for modular task allocation in autonomous mobile systems and report state-of-the-art performance on multi-robot dispatching benchmarks; Dhanaraj et al. (2025) study proactive contingency-aware task allocation and scheduling in multi-robot multi-human cells via hindsight optimization; Wen & Ma (2024) develop an indicator-based evolutionary algorithm for multi-objective multi-robot task allocation; Wesselhöft et al. (2022) survey reinforcement-learning approaches to AMR fleet control and identify six open challenges, including the sim-to-real gap and data efficiency. These approaches obtain policies without an explicit elevator-model commitment and would, in principle, sidestep the model uncertainty problem that our Hedge Rule addresses. We see two reasons the analytical route is complementary rather than dominated. First, the diagnostic value of our Bound-and-Gap decomposition does not transfer to learned policies, which optimize a single scalar objective and do not expose the partition-intrinsic-versus-recoverable split. Second, the closed-form Hedge Rule requires no training data and provides a deployment-ready reference against which any learned policy can be measured. The two paradigms, therefore, answer different questions about the same physical system.

Three threads of prior methodological work inform our analytical apparatus: the first two are connected to our tools by precise equivalences established in Section 4, and the third by a structural contrast. First, the prediction-to-decision regret literature (Chenreddy & Delage, 2024; Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2020; Vera et al., 2020) bounds the algorithmic loss when a learned predictor drives a downstream optimizer. Theorem 1 in Section 4.1.2 gives this regret a partition perspective: the policy-slack component MΦ​ of our decomposition equals the Smart-Predict-then-Optimize regret of the partition-constant predictor class, so the literature’s training-time regret object and our post-hoc, structured-feature decomposition refer to the same underlying quantity on a finite partition. Our contribution is the decomposition itself, which separates this SPO regret from an independent partition-intrinsic headroom term Hup​. Second, Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization (Blanchet & Murthy, 2019; Gao & Kleywegt, 2023; Mohajerin Esfahani & Kuhn, 2018) hedges decisions against worst-case distributions within a transport-distance ambiguity ball of a nominal model, typically reformulated as a convex program; the underlying robust-optimization framework dates to Delage & Ye (2010) and is surveyed by Bertsimas et al. (2011). Theorem 2 in Section 4.2.2 shows that under a chain of per-wave stochastic dominance conditions, our minimax wave-corner selection over {M1, M2, M3} coincides with the Wasserstein distributionally robust solution at the appropriate ambiguity radius, collapsing the convex program into a closed-form one-line rule. Our contribution is the dominance-conditional collapse, which exploits an empirically verified structural property to replace optimisation with identification. Third, robust scheduling under model uncertainty (Lu & Shen, 2021; Wiesemann et al., 2013) hedges against parametric variation within a single agreed-upon model class. Our elevator-modelling uncertainty is structurally different: throughput aggregation and true co-occupancy batching are qualitatively distinct models, not parameterizations of one family. The Model-Dominance Hedge Rule addresses this structural model-class disagreement rather than parametric uncertainty within a class.

Collectively, the four problem-class threads each focus on a different aspect of wave-elevator coupling. This paper formalizes the integration of four-way interaction, wave composition, multi-story deployment, flexible AMR fleets, and shared elevator capacity into a single decision problem. The methodological threads provide analytical grounding for our two tools: the first two supply the frameworks our equivalence theorems extend, and the third positions them against a competing approach. The empirical findings demonstrating these contributions in action are deferred to Sections 5 and 6.
